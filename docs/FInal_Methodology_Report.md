This study investigates whether sentiment extracted from professional financial news headlines can improve short-term stock price direction prediction, either independently or when combined with basic technical indicators. The analysis is framed as a next-day prediction task in order to align with existing literature on short-term market predictability and to avoid ambiguity regarding intraday timing and feature availability. The prediction objective is defined as determining whether the closing price of a stock on trading day t+1 is greater than its closing price on day t. This task is formulated as a binary classification problem, where a value of 1 indicates a positive price movement and a value of 0 indicates no increase. All predictive features are constructed using information available by the close of trading on day t, ensuring that no future information is incorporated into the modeling process.

Stock price data for Apple Inc. (AAPL) were obtained from Yahoo Finance. Daily historical data include open, high, low, close, and volume values and were used to construct the target labels and technical indicator features. Yahoo Finance was selected due to its reliability, transparency, and widespread use in academic research. All price-based features were computed exclusively from historical values available up to and including day t when predicting the price movement on day t+1. The analysis period spans one calendar year, and only trading days are included.

News sentiment data were derived from Wall Street Journal articles accessed through the ProQuest academic database. Only articles with Apple-related terms appearing explicitly in the headline were included, ensuring that the sentiment signal was directly relevant to the target asset. Sentiment analysis was performed at the headline level rather than using full article text in order to reduce noise and reflect the type of information most immediately consumed by market participants. Headlines were grouped by publication date and aggregated to a daily level prior to model input.

Sentiment extraction was performed using FinBERT, a transformer-based language model pre-trained on financial text. Each headline was classified into one of three sentiment categories: positive, negative, or neutral, with the model outputting a probability distribution across these classes. Rather than relying on a discrete sentiment label, the probability outputs were retained for feature engineering. For each trading day, individual headline sentiment probabilities were aggregated using the mean across all headlines published on that day. This produced three daily sentiment features: average positive sentiment probability, average negative sentiment probability, and average neutral sentiment probability. These features preserve information about sentiment intensity and uncertainty while avoiding arbitrary thresholding. The mapping between FinBERT output indices and sentiment categories was explicitly verified to ensure correct polarity interpretation.

Sentiment and price data were aligned using an inner join on trading dates. Only dates for which both stock price data and at least one Wall Street Journal headline were available were retained. As a result, non-trading days such as weekends and market holidays were excluded, even if headlines were published on those dates. This approach avoids assumptions about carrying sentiment forward across non-trading periods and ensures that each observation corresponds to a valid trading day with both sentiment and price information.

To complement sentiment features, a deliberately small and interpretable set of technical indicators was constructed using historical price data from Yahoo Finance. Feature selection was intentionally constrained to reduce overfitting risk and to maintain methodological transparency. The first indicator is the one-day return, defined as the percentage change in closing price from day t-1 to day t. This feature captures short-term momentum and is fully known by the close of trading on day t, making it suitable for next-day prediction without introducing look-ahead bias. The second indicator measures the relative distance of the current closing price from its 20-day simple moving average, capturing deviations from recent price trends and potential mean-reversion behavior. The third indicator is the day-over-day change in the 14-day Relative Strength Index, which captures momentum acceleration or deceleration rather than absolute momentum levels. All technical indicators were computed using price data available up to and including day t, and no feature incorporates information from day t+1 or later.

Sentiment and technical features were merged at the daily level and aligned with the target variable such that feature vectors from day t were used to predict price movement from day t to day t+1. The target label was constructed by applying a forward shift to the closing price series, while all feature columns remained unshifted. Rows with missing values resulting from rolling window calculations were removed. After alignment and preprocessing, the final dataset consisted of 143 trading-day observations.

Three feature configurations were evaluated using the same dataset and evaluation protocol. The first configuration uses only aggregated daily sentiment features derived from Wall Street Journal headlines to assess whether news sentiment alone contains predictive information. The second configuration uses only the selected technical indicators derived from historical price data and serves as a traditional technical baseline. The third configuration combines sentiment and technical features to evaluate whether sentiment provides incremental predictive value beyond technical signals. All experiments use Logistic Regression as the sole classification model. Logistic Regression was selected due to its interpretability, suitability for small sample sizes, and common use as a baseline model in financial prediction research. The focus of the analysis is on feature comparison rather than model selection.

To respect the temporal structure of financial time series data, all train-test splits were performed chronologically. The dataset was divided into a training set comprising the first 70 percent of observations (100 samples) and a test set comprising the remaining 30 percent (43 samples). No random shuffling was applied. Feature scaling was performed using standardization, with scaling parameters fit exclusively on the training data and then applied to the test data to prevent data leakage.

In addition to the holdout test, 5-fold time-series cross-validation was performed to provide more robust performance estimates and assess model stability across different time periods. Cross-validation was conducted using TimeSeriesSplit, which maintains temporal ordering by creating expanding training windows and sequential test sets. This approach ensures that training data always precedes test data chronologically, preventing look-ahead bias while allowing evaluation across multiple time periods. Performance metrics from cross-validation are reported as mean values with standard deviations across the five folds, providing insight into the consistency of model performance across different temporal segments of the dataset.

Model performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Accuracy was interpreted cautiously and used primarily as a diagnostic measure rather than an indicator of trading profitability. Because all models were evaluated on the same test set, McNemar's test was applied to paired prediction outcomes to assess whether differences in classification accuracy between feature configurations were statistically significant. Due to the limited sample size, statistical power is acknowledged as a limitation, and non-significant results are interpreted conservatively.

Multiple safeguards were implemented to prevent data leakage throughout the experimental pipeline. Forward time shifts were applied only to the target variable, all features were computed using information available by market close on day t, feature scaling was fit exclusively on training data, and chronological splits were used consistently across all experiments. These measures ensure that reported results reflect realistic predictive performance rather than artifacts of improper data handling.

In summary, this methodology provides a transparent and reproducible framework for evaluating the predictive value of financial news sentiment and technical indicators for next-day stock price direction. By restricting feature complexity, enforcing strict temporal alignment, and employing conservative evaluation practices, the study aims to provide an honest assessment of whether professional news sentiment offers incremental predictive value within the context of short-term market efficiency.
